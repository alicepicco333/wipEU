Women in Power and Decision-Making in the EU · an open data project, 2023/24

How do the levels of representation of women in different decision-making positions correlate within the European Union?

Is there a discernible pattern or trend in the relationships between women occupying various roles, and how have these correlations evolved over time?

02

Approach

wipEU looks at how the presence of women in positions of power and decision-making in the EU relates across different roles, and how that relationship has changed over time.

The project examines the factors that shape who holds influence in the EU, with an emphasis on how these dynamics develop across different periods: progress, persistent gaps and the trends behind them. The aim is to add evidence to discussions, policies and actions for gender equity in European decision-making. Alongside the research question, the project examines open data itself: its quality, legality, ethics and technical form.

Findings

Every area moved towards parity. None reached it.

Share of women in one headline role per area, EU level. The line at 50% marks parity.

Change since 2017

Percentage-point change from 2017 to the latest year EIGE published for each series (2023, or 2022 for environment ministries). Politics, finance and environment gained ten points or more; the agencies and the Court of Justice moved about four.

The smallest bodies move in large steps. The three EU financial institutions (ECB, EIB, EIF) had no woman as president or chair in any year from 2003 to 2019; one in 2020, 2021 and 2022; two of three in 2023.

The higher the seat, the fewer the women.

Show the values as a table

Each row compares two levels of the same body in the latest year EIGE published both. Where the top level has only a handful of seats (the three EU financial institutions, the presidents of the EU courts), one appointment moves its share by 30 points or more; those rows are drawn with a dashed link.

04

Do the roles move together?

Only loosely. A country with many women in one top role often has few in another.

This is the question the project started from: if a country has many women in one kind of top job, does it also have many in the others? Take party leaders and the presidents of research-funding boards. If the countries with the most women leading parties were also the ones with the most women chairing research boards, the two roles would “move together”. We checked every pair of six roles across the 27 EU countries.

  1. Each square compares two roles across the 27 EU countries: the role on its row and the role in its column.
  2. The number is a match score from −1 to +1. Near +1, the same countries have many women in both roles. Near 0, there is no link. Below 0, countries with many women in one role tend to have few in the other.
  3. Outlined squares are strong enough not to be a coincidence. Select any square to see the countries behind it on the right.

−1 opposite0 no link+1 same countries Outlined: clearly linked, not a coincidence
Show every pair and year as a table

How to read this, and its limits

The match score is Spearman's rank correlation (ρ): the countries are put in order by the share of women in each role, and the score says how similar the two orders are. It shows whether countries line up, not whether one role causes the other. With about 27 countries, only scores beyond roughly ±0.38 are unlikely to come from chance alone (5% level); those squares are outlined.

Pace to parity

If the last ten years simply continued.

A projection, not a prediction: the year each headline series would cross 50% if it kept the average yearly change of its last ten published years.

Show the projection as a table

How the projection is made, and why to distrust it

Method. For each series we take the last ten years EIGE published (fewer where the series is shorter: research funding starts in 2017), fit an ordinary least-squares straight line to the yearly share of women, and solve for the year the line reaches 50%. The dashed line in each panel is that fit, extended. Where the slope is zero or negative the answer is “not reached”. Projections stop at 2100.

Limits. Appointments come in waves, bodies change size, and a straight line has no reason to hold: the fit ignores elections, quotas and mandates. The research funding series jumps in 2018 when EIGE's coverage grew from 628 to 841 board members. Treat each year as a way of describing the recent pace, not as a forecast.

09

Method & analyses

How the numbers are made

  1. Source. Six datasets downloaded in January 2024 from the Women and men in decision-making section of EIGE's Gender Statistics Database, as .csv and .json. Each was split into women, men and total tables: 18 source files.
  2. Share. For every body, role and year: share of women = women ÷ total headcount × 100, rounded half up to one decimal. The script scripts/build_web_data.py checks each value against EIGE's published percentage: all match.
  3. Change. The difference between two shares, in percentage points (pts), for example 26.8% − 16.0% = +10.8 pts.
  4. Seats. The chamber draws one dot for every person in EIGE's headcount for that body and year; it adds nothing and rounds nothing. “Seats short of parity” is the number of extra women needed for women to hold at least half: ⌈total ÷ 2⌉ − women.
  5. Movers. The change in share, in percentage points, between 2017 and the selected year for the chosen dataset and role, for countries with a value in both years. In bodies of four to eight people, one appointment moves a country by 12 to 25 points.
  6. EU level. Country datasets use EIGE's EU-27 (from 2020) aggregate. Institution datasets use EIGE's Total across bodies; the Court of Justice series uses the European Court of Justice alone.

Two mashup datasets

The team built two mashups, separated by the parameter that organises them. MD1 joins the country-level data (space): countries are the rows, suited to maps and spatial analysis. MD2 joins the organisation-level data (time): EU agencies and bodies are the rows, suited to timelines and change over time. Keeping them apart let each follow its own structure and visualisation technique, geoJSON and choropleths for one and temporal charts for the other, while leaving room to combine the spatial and temporal views of EU structures.

Correlations and clusters

Correlation measures how strongly two variables change together; it does not imply causation. The team used Spearman's rank correlation, which captures monotonic relationships without assuming a straight line. Clustering groups data points with similar characteristics, here countries and agencies with similar temporal patterns. The documentation reports high correlations within the same category across years, varied correlations between categories in the same year, and some negative year-to-year correlations, where a rise in one category coincides with a fall in another. Section 04 recomputes the country-level correlations for every year, directly from the EIGE headcounts.

Four analyses of the data

Together these support the trustworthiness, usability and ethical handling of the EIGE data for research, policymaking and advocacy. Full reports are in the documentation.

What the data cannot show

The datasets record only women and men. That binary leaves out queer and non-binary people, whose presence in decision-making is not measured at all. Data reflects the categories of the society that collects it, and those categories can marginalise the people they fail to count. This limit is a prompt to ask not only what is measured but what is left unmeasured, and to call for more inclusive data collection.
